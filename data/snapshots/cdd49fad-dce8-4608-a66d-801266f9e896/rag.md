# Self-Disclosure and Liking

## Source

- Authors: Nancy L. Collins and Lynn Carol Miller
- Title: *Self-Disclosure and Liking: A Meta-Analytic Review*
- Journal: *Psychological Bulletin*, 1994, 116(3), 457–475
- DOI: 10.1037/0033-2909.116.3.457
- Public record: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7809308/

## What the review examined

This meta-analytic review evaluated research on the relationship between self-disclosure and liking. It separated three related possibilities: people who disclose more intimately may be liked more; people may disclose more to someone they already like; and the act of disclosing may itself increase liking for the listener.

The review reported statistically meaningful relationships for all three patterns. It also found that the strength and meaning of the relationship varied with features such as the research design, the kind of disclosure, and characteristics of the person disclosing.

## Claims this source can support

- Self-disclosure is associated with the development and maintenance of interpersonal relationships.
- Disclosure and liking can influence each other in more than one direction.
- Revealing personal information can contribute to connection, but the context and type of disclosure matter.
- A simple claim that "more disclosure always creates more intimacy" is not supported by the review's moderated results.

## What this source does not establish

- Association does not mean that every disclosure causes trust or relationship success.
- The review does not support pressuring someone to reveal private information.
- Disclosure used strategically to manufacture obligation is not equivalent to reciprocal intimacy.
- The findings do not prove that rapid or unlimited disclosure is healthier than gradual disclosure.

## Useful debate distinction

The evidence can support Adrian's claim that revealing something real may help attraction become personal. It also gives an opponent grounds to ask whether disclosure is reciprocal, voluntary, well-paced, and met with care rather than merely used as a technique.

# Novel Shared Activities and Experienced Relationship Quality

## Source

- Authors: Arthur Aron, Christina C. Norman, Elaine N. Aron, Colin McKenna, and Richard E. Heyman
- Title: *Couples' Shared Participation in Novel and Arousing Activities and Experienced Relationship Quality*
- Journal: *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 2000, 78(2), 273–284
- DOI: 10.1037/0022-3514.78.2.273
- Public record: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10707334/

## What the research examined

The authors examined whether couples' participation in shared activities that were experienced as novel and arousing was associated with changes in experienced relationship quality. The publication reported evidence from questionnaire and survey data as well as three laboratory experiments.

The survey findings linked reports of shared exciting activities with relationship satisfaction and examined relationship boredom as a possible explanatory pathway. In the laboratory studies, couples who completed a brief novel and arousing task together showed a larger immediate increase in experienced relationship quality than couples assigned to a more ordinary task. A no-activity comparison supported the interpretation that the change was connected to the novel shared task rather than measurement alone.

## Claims this source can support

- Shared novelty can contribute to how partners experience relationship quality in the short term.
- Maintaining a relationship may involve creating new shared experiences rather than relying only on verbal discussion.
- Boredom and routine are relevant when considering why excitement may decline.
- Attraction and excitement are not necessarily enemies of a stable relationship.

## What this source does not establish

- It does not show that novelty alone creates trust, commitment, honesty, or long-term compatibility.
- A short laboratory increase in experienced relationship quality is not proof of a permanent relationship improvement.
- The findings do not justify manufactured jealousy, deception, emotional uncertainty, unsafe risk-taking, or coercion.
- The publication does not imply that every couple prefers the same type or intensity of activity.

## Useful debate distinction

This evidence supports a bounded argument that shared novelty can refresh experienced relationship quality. It should be combined with separate evidence when making claims about trust, attachment, conflict repair, or long-term stability.

# TikTok Recommendation Governance: Safety, Expression, and Shared Discovery

> **Teaching corpus:** `formal-tiktok-recommendation-governance-v1`
> **Language:** English
> **Evidence freeze:** 2026-08-30 (Asia/Shanghai)
> **Classroom question:** Should the United States ban TikTok's recommendation algorithm?

## How to read this brief

This is an original classroom synthesis of public materials. It is designed to let
students distinguish a documented fact from a platform statement, a legal rule, and
a value judgment. Bracketed IDs such as `C01` map to the claim ledger in
`source-manifest.json`; they are provenance markers, not footnotes to be read as a
court endorsement of every policy conclusion.

The evidence is bounded by the dates in the manifest. A page that describes a
platform's own product or policy is labelled as a **platform statement**. It is not
an independent audit of what the platform does in every market. The legal material is
limited to the United States statute enacted in 2024 and the Supreme Court opinion
issued on 2025-01-17. This brief is not legal advice, a prediction of later
enforcement, or a claim that any source proves a causal mental-health effect.

## 1. What is being governed?

**Documented description.** The Supreme Court described TikTok's For You page as a
personalized feed. It said that interactions such as watching, following, and
commenting give the recommendation system information with which to tailor a feed;
content moderation and other promotion or demotion decisions also shape what a user
sees. `[C01]` The Court also described the recommendation algorithm as proprietary
and discussed ByteDance's role in its ownership and development in the context of the
government's national-security concerns. `[C02]` These descriptions explain why the
policy question is about control over distribution and data, not simply about whether
short videos are good or bad.

**Platform statement.** TikTok's support material says that recommendations can use
user interactions, video information such as captions, sounds, and hashtags, and
device or account settings. It says stronger signals, such as finishing a longer
video, generally carry more weight than weaker contextual signals, such as a shared
country between viewer and creator. `[C03]` TikTok also describes controls such as
“Why this post,” “Not interested,” feed refresh, keyword filters, and topic
preferences. `[C04]` Those statements make user agency a testable governance goal,
but they do not disclose the complete ranking code or independently establish how
well each control works.

## 2. The legal choice is narrower than “ban the app”

The Protecting Americans from Foreign Adversary Controlled Applications Act creates a
prohibition on covered distribution, maintenance, updating, or hosting services for a
foreign-adversary-controlled application, while allowing an exception for a
**qualified divestiture**. `[C05]` The statutory definition requires ending foreign
adversary control and ending specified operational relationships, including continuing
relationships concerning the content recommendation algorithm or data sharing.
`[C06]` A student can therefore ask two separate questions: whether the security risk
is concrete, and whether credible separation or oversight can reduce it without a
broader prohibition.

The Supreme Court's per curiam opinion recognized TikTok as an outlet for expression
and concluded that the challenged provisions do not violate First Amendment rights on
the record before it. `[C07]` Justice Sotomayor's concurrence separately described
compiling and curating material as expressive activity. The opinion is a
decision about a particular statute, parties, and evidentiary record. It does not
establish that every recommendation service is controlled in the same way, nor that a
ban is the only lawful response to a security concern.

## 3. Youth protection has several layers

**Platform policy.** TikTok's under-13 support page says that people below 13 in the
United States are directed to a separate experience. It describes a private account,
age-appropriate For You and Discover feeds, a one-hour screen-time limit, device-only
saving of created videos, and no commenting, sharing, or messaging. `[C08]` TikTok's
under-18 privacy guidance says that ages 13–15 start with private accounts and that
some features, including direct messaging, are unavailable until a later age; it
also notes that local law can change the applicable setting. `[C09]` These are
company-reported safeguards, so a governance proposal should require measurable
transparency about coverage, error rates, appeals, and regional differences.

**Public-law boundary.** The Federal Trade Commission explains that the Children's
Online Privacy Protection Rule (COPPA) concerns operators collecting personal
information from children under 13 and requires verifiable parental consent in the
covered circumstances. `[C10]` COPPA is a privacy baseline, not a complete youth
recommendation-safety regime. It should not be used to claim that all teenagers are
covered by the same legal threshold or that parental consent resolves ranking risks.

**Evidence limit.** A youth-protection argument may reasonably value age-appropriate
design, privacy, and control. This corpus does not claim that a specific amount of
TikTok use causes a particular health outcome. A careful proposal asks what harm is
being addressed, which age group is affected, how the safeguard would be tested, and
what remedy is available when age estimation or moderation is wrong.

## 4. Expression, innovation, and cultural exchange

The Court's opinion recognized that TikTok is used by a very large U.S. audience, while
Justice Sotomayor's concurrence described the service's compilation and curation of
speech as expressive activity. `[C11]` For a creator, recommendation can be a route to an audience that is not already following
them; for a viewer, it can reveal a language, art form, or civic perspective that a
friend-only feed would not surface. Those benefits are a **value and design
inference**, not a finding that a particular ranking outcome is fair or innovative.

TikTok says that it tries to diversify recommendations by introducing people to new
creators, ideas, and perspectives, and that its system can show content outside a
user's expressed interests. `[C12]` This supports a useful innovation question: does
the system give newcomers and minority-language creators a meaningful chance to be
discovered, or does opaque optimization reward already-proven engagement? Students
should demand exposure and appeal data before turning either answer into a factual
claim.

UNESCO's 2023 Guidelines for the Governance of Digital Platforms call for freedom of
expression and access to information alongside platform transparency, accountability,
human-rights due diligence, and protection of cultural diversity. `[C13]` The
Guidelines also describe media and information literacy as a way to empower users and
support cultural diversity, social inclusion, and global citizenship. `[C14]` They
are a governance framework, not a TikTok audit or a command to choose one country's
regulatory model. A cross-cultural design should therefore ask who defines “harm,”
whose language and norms are represented in review, and whether users can see and
contest a recommendation or moderation decision.

## 5. Compare policy options without manufacturing agreement

The following options are proposals for discussion. The proposal labels mark values
and institutional choices; they are not facts found in the sources.

| Option | What it could protect | What it could cost or risk | Questions for evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Qualified divestiture with verified separation | Reduce foreign-control and data-sharing risk while retaining a service | Separation may be difficult to verify; a new owner may still optimize opaquely | Who controls the algorithm, updates, data access, and audits after separation? |
| Independent audits, transparency, and user controls | Improve contestability, youth safeguards, and accountability without removing speech | Audits can be narrow, delayed, or unable to inspect trade secrets; controls can burden users | What data, tests, and appeal outcomes are disclosed, and to whom? |
| Age-appropriate defaults and privacy limits | Reduce exposure and unwanted contact for children and teens | Age assurance can be inaccurate or intrusive; restrictions can limit young creators | Are error rates, regional rules, and remedies public? |
| A broad prohibition on the covered recommendation service | Avoid the identified control relationship during the prohibition period | Removes a channel for expression, experimentation, commerce, and cross-cultural discovery | Is the risk specific and substantial, and why are narrower safeguards insufficient? |

There is no rule that a sound classroom exchange must produce a disagreement. One
student may agree that a security risk is serious while extending the proposal with
independent oversight. Another may challenge the proportionality of a prohibition
while qualifying that position for cases where separation cannot be verified. The
important distinction is between a source-backed claim, a transparent inference, and
a value trade-off.

## 6. A disciplined recommendation template

Before recommending a policy, state:

1. **The risk:** identify the control, data, youth-safety, expression, or cultural
   concern, and cite the narrow source-backed proposition.
2. **The affected people:** include users, minors, creators, reviewers, and
   cross-border communities rather than treating “the public” as one interest.
3. **The test:** name the evidence that would show the safeguard works, including
   regional and language coverage, false positives, and appeals.
4. **The fallback:** explain what happens if separation, audit, age assurance, or
   transparency fails.
5. **The limit:** record an uncertainty or value conflict that remains unresolved.

A defensible classroom conclusion may be conditional: preserve access if verified
separation and independent accountability meet published tests; use narrower youth and
privacy safeguards where those address the concrete harm; and reserve a prohibition
for a demonstrated risk that cannot be controlled proportionately. That is a policy
judgment, not a majority vote disguised as fact.

# TikTok Recommendation Algorithm: Evidence for a Classroom Policy Debate

> Test fixture status: prepared for Wave 2 manual RAG acceptance. It paraphrases public legal sources available in 2024–2025. It is not the final course evidence pack and does not state the platform's current operating status in August 2026.

## What the recommendation algorithm does

The U.S. Supreme Court described TikTok's “For You” page as a personalized feed produced by a proprietary recommendation algorithm. User interactions—such as watching a video, following an account, or leaving a comment—help the system tailor later recommendations. The Court also described content moderation, promotion, and demotion as additional forces shaping what a user sees. This means the debate concerns both data-driven personalization and control over the distribution of attention.

Source: Supreme Court of the United States, *TikTok Inc. v. Garland*, 604 U.S. 56 (2025), pp. 2–3.  
https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/24pdf/24-656_new_3dq3.pdf

## Ownership and operational-control concern

The Supreme Court opinion stated that TikTok Inc.'s ultimate parent is ByteDance Ltd.; that ByteDance owns TikTok's proprietary algorithm; and that the algorithm was developed and maintained in China. The opinion presented these ownership and control facts as part of the background to the U.S. government's national-security concerns. A policy argument can therefore focus on who can direct the recommendation system and access relevant data, rather than treating every foreign-owned application as equally risky.

Source: Supreme Court of the United States, *TikTok Inc. v. Garland*, 604 U.S. 56 (2025), pp. 3–4.  
https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/24pdf/24-656_new_3dq3.pdf

## The law used divestiture rather than an unconditional permanent ban

The Protecting Americans from Foreign Adversary Controlled Applications Act prohibited covered U.S. distribution, maintenance, updating, and hosting services for a foreign-adversary-controlled application, but created an exemption when a qualified divestiture occurred. Its definition of qualified divestiture required ending foreign-adversary control and preventing continuing operational relationships involving the content recommendation algorithm or data sharing. This structure supports a more precise debate question: whether prohibition is justified until credible separation is achieved, not whether short-video communication itself should disappear.

Source: U.S. Congress, H.R. 7521, 118th Congress, introduced text, sections 2(a), 2(c), and 2(g)(6).  
https://www.congress.gov/118/bills/hr7521/BILLS-118hr7521ih.pdf

## Government rationale is a claim to evaluate, not an automatic conclusion

After the D.C. Circuit ruling, the U.S. Department of Justice said the law was intended to prevent collection of sensitive information and covert manipulation of content delivered to American audiences. This is the government's stated rationale. A careful speaker should identify it as the government's position and still ask what evidence, oversight, or institutional safeguards are necessary to justify a restriction.

Source: U.S. Department of Justice, statement dated December 6, 2024 and updated February 6, 2025.  
https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/justice-department-statements-us-court-appeals-district-columbia-circuit-ruling-tiktok-et-al

## The strongest trade-off: expression and cultural participation

The Supreme Court recognized that TikTok was used by more than 170 million people in the United States and that the case involved technologies with transformative expressive capacity. The Court's decision was narrowly focused on the specific record and law before it. A prohibition can therefore impose serious costs on creators, audiences, businesses, and cross-cultural communication even when the government identifies a genuine security concern. A strong policy should explain why narrower safeguards are insufficient, or adopt qualified divestiture and independent oversight when those measures can address the risk.

Source: Supreme Court of the United States, *TikTok Inc. v. Garland*, 604 U.S. 56 (2025), pp. 1–2 and 15–19.  
https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/24pdf/24-656_new_3dq3.pdf

## Questions this evidence can support

- Who controls the recommendation algorithm and relevant data?
- Is the policy aimed at speech, ownership, data access, algorithmic control, or some combination?
- Would qualified divestiture, independent auditing, data separation, transparency, or user control address the risk?
- What burdens would a prohibition place on expression, creators, commerce, and cultural exchange?
- What evidence would justify choosing prohibition over narrower safeguards?

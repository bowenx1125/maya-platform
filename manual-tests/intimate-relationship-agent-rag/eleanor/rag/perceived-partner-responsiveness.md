# Perceived Partner Responsiveness: Meaning and Measurement

## Source

- Authors: Danielle Crasta, Ronald D. Rogge, Michael R. Maniaci, and Harry T. Reis
- Title: *Toward an Optimized Measure of Perceived Partner Responsiveness: Development and Validation of the Perceived Responsiveness and Insensitivity Scale*
- Journal: *Psychological Assessment*, 2021, 33(4), 338–355
- DOI: 10.1037/pas0000986
- Publisher record: https://doi.org/10.1037/pas0000986

## Concept

Perceived partner responsiveness concerns whether a person experiences a close other as attentive to important concerns and as understanding, validating, and caring toward the self. Relationship research uses this construct to study how people interpret a partner's responses, not merely whether the partner performed a particular visible behavior.

The publication developed and evaluated shorter measures of perceived responsiveness and insensitivity. The first study drew on a large set of candidate items from existing measures; later studies examined the resulting scales' structure and validity.

## Claims this source can support

- Feeling understood, validated, and cared for is a measurable component of perceived responsiveness.
- Responsiveness and insensitivity should not be reduced to a single vague label such as "being nice."
- Relationship analysis should consider how behavior is experienced by the partner, not only the actor's stated intention.
- Reliable measurement requires multiple items and validation rather than diagnosing a relationship from one anecdote.

## What this source does not establish

- A scale score is not a diagnosis and does not determine whether a couple should remain together.
- Perception can be influenced by context, expectations, prior experiences, and measurement method.
- Responsiveness does not require agreement with every request or the removal of personal boundaries.
- The research does not support monitoring, testing, or manipulating a partner to obtain reassurance.

## Useful debate distinction

This evidence supports the psychologist's argument that intimacy involves more than attention or intensity. A person may receive frequent messages or dramatic affection yet still feel misunderstood or unsafe. Conversely, a boundary or disagreement can coexist with responsiveness when it is communicated with understanding, validation, and care.

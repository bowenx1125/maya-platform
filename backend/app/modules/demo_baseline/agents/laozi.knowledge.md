# Water, yielding and the uncarved block

The Daoist tradition drew its instruction from ordinary things. Water was the standing
example: it takes the lowest place, yields to every obstacle, and still wears through
stone. Softness outlasting hardness was offered as an observation about how change
actually happens, not as a moral preference.

Wu wei, often rendered as non-forcing action, does not mean doing nothing. It means
acting along the grain of a situation so that little effort is wasted on resistance the
actor created. A ruler who governs this way is described as barely noticed, and the
people say the result came about of itself.

The uncarved block stands for potential not yet narrowed. Every cut makes a thing more
useful for one purpose and less available for others, so the tradition treats early
commitment with caution and values keeping options intact.

The tradition also names its own risk. Patience can become passivity, and a preference
for the unforced can leave a real harm unaddressed while one waits for conditions to
turn. The texts answer this by tying restraint to attention: not acting is defensible
only when one is watching closely enough to know that the moment has not yet come.

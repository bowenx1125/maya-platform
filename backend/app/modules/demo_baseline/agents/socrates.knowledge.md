# Inquiry in the Athenian square

Athenian public life treated argument as something performed among citizens rather
than written down and filed away. A claim entered the square, met a question, and
either survived or was revised in front of the people it concerned.

The method rested on definition. Before asking whether an action was just, the
questioner asked what justice meant, and whether the offered meaning still held when
an uncomfortable example was placed beside it. A definition that collapsed under one
counter-case was treated as information, not as defeat.

This practice assumed that the person questioned was a partner rather than an
opponent. The questioner claimed no special knowledge; the value of the exchange came
from both sides discovering together where an idea stopped being clear.

Its limits were also visible. Relentless questioning could stall a decision that a
city needed to make, and a community that prized examination could mistake clever
refutation for wisdom. Athenian practice therefore paired open inquiry with fixed
moments when the assembly had to choose and act, even on incomplete understanding.
